Finding the unusual in data: from one-feature rules to Isolation Forest, One-Class SVM and Local Outlier Factor, told through one marketplace's review queue.

Opening story

The listing that was too good to be true

DriveDeal is an online marketplace for used cars. Sellers post a listing with a handful of details, and DriveDeal's pricing engine suggests a fair price from the car's mileage and what similar cars sell for.

Used electric cars have started to appear. There aren't many yet, and they behave differently from petrol and diesel cars. Nandini, who heads listing quality, calls Rohan, a data analyst, to her desk.

"Last week we had three problems in one day. A listing with a mileage of 1,000,000, which is obviously a typo. A four-year-old car priced like a brand-new one, which looked like a scam. And a pricing suggestion for an EV that was wildly wrong, because our engine has never really seen cars like it."

"I have three reviewers. They can check maybe a few dozen listings a day. I need a ranked list of the listings that look unusual. But not everything unusual is bad. Typos and scams I want to fix or remove. Genuinely new kinds of cars I want to learn from, not delete."

Problem 1

Spotting data that is wrong or suspicious.

Problem 2

Spotting data that is real but new.

The catch

Nobody has ever labelled a listing "anomaly" or "normal". The family of methods built for exactly this is anomaly detection.
Part 1

Anomaly, outlier, novelty

These words are often used interchangeably, but the distinctions are useful. The difference lies in the reference data.

Explorer · three terms

Anomaly is the broad term: an observation that does not fit the normal pattern.

Your training data may contain anomalies, and the job is to find them in that data. Examples: cleaning a dataset, or finding suspicious listings in last month's data.

scikit-learn fit_predict on the data itself.

Your training data is assumed to be clean, and the job is to flag new observations that look unlike it. Example: screening each incoming listing against approved history.

scikit-learn fit on clean data, then predict on new points. LocalOutlierFactor offers novelty=True for this mode.

Kinds of anomaly

Explorer · three kinds

A single observation is unusual by itself. Example: a mileage of 1,000,000.

Normal in general, but unusual in context. Example: a high price is normal for a luxury car, but not for a 12-year-old hatchback.

Each observation looks fine, but a group together is unusual. Example: a burst of near-identical listings from one seller.

Here we mostly look at point and contextual anomalies using the combination of Mileage and Price.

Part 3

Simple detectors and their limits

IQR and Z-score both look at one feature at a time.

IQR rule

With Q1, Q3 the 25th and 75th percentiles, IQR = Q3 − Q1. Flag a value below Q1 − 1.5·IQR or above Q3 + 1.5·IQR. It works from percentiles, so it copes with skewed data.

Z-score rule

z = (x − x̄) / s
Counts how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. A common rule flags |z| > 3. It suits roughly bell-shaped data.
Result on DriveDeal listings

Flagged counts per feature out of 908 listings. These rules catch listings that are extreme in a single feature. They have two weaknesses.

Code: IQR and Z-score flags
def iqr_flags(s, k=1.5):
    q1, q3 = s.quantile([0.25, 0.75])
    iqr = q3 - q1
    return (s < q1 - k * iqr) | (s > q3 + k * iqr)

def z_flags(s, thr=3):
    return ((s - s.mean()) / s.std()).abs() > thr

flag_iqr = pd.DataFrame({c: iqr_flags(data[c]) for c in data.columns})
flag_z   = pd.DataFrame({c: z_flags(data[c])   for c in data.columns})
uni = flag_iqr.any(axis=1) | flag_z.any(axis=1)

Weakness 1: masking

The mean and standard deviation are themselves pulled by the outliers. An extreme value inflates the standard deviation, which shrinks its own z-score.

Takeaway. Adding two extremes raises both the mean and the standard deviation, so their ordinary z-scores stay below 3 and the rule misses them. A listing can be ordinary in Mileage and ordinary in Price, yet impossible as a pair: a car with very high mileage and a very high price, or very low mileage and a very low price. Each value is inside the normal range of its feature, but not together.

Part 4

Isolation Forest: unusual points are easy to separate

Imagine playing "20 questions" with random questions to pick one listing out of the crowd. A listing in a dense crowd needs many questions. A listing far from the crowd can be separated in a few.

An isolation tree

  1. Pick a random feature.
  2. Pick a random split value between that feature's minimum and maximum.
  3. Split the data into two sides, and repeat on each side.
  4. Stop when a point is alone (or at a depth limit).

The depth at which a point ends up alone (its path length) measures how easy it was to isolate. Unusual points are isolated sooner (shallow), normal points later (deep).

Interactive · play the isolation game

Pick a target. Random cuts keep only the side containing it, until it is alone.

Illustrative synthetic points. Choose a target to start.

From one tree to a forest

An Isolation Forest builds many such random trees, each on a small random sub-sample of the data (256 points by default). A point's score uses its average depth across the trees:

s(x) = 2−E[h(x)] / c(ψ)

E[h(x)] is the average path length and c(ψ) is a normaliser: the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a tree built on ψ points. Sub-sampling is not a train/test split. It simply keeps each tree small and cheap.

Building a small Isolation Forest from scratch

Step explorer

The score compares a point's depth with the depth we would expect for a typical point. That expected depth is c(n). For n ≤ 1 it is 0, for n = 2 it is 1, and otherwise 2·(ln(n−1)+γ) − 2(n−1)/n, where γ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. It is used twice: to fix the depth of leaves that still hold several points, and to scale the final score.

EULER = 0.5772156649
def c_factor(n):
    if n <= 1: return 0.0
    if n == 2: return 1.0
    return 2 * (np.log(n - 1) + EULER) - 2 * (n - 1) / n

build_tree plays "20 questions" on one random sub-sample. It stops with a leaf if the tree is deep enough or one point is left. Otherwise it picks a random feature, finds its min and max (no spread means a leaf), picks a random threshold between them, sends points below it left and the rest right, and calls itself on each side with depth + 1 (recursion). A node is ("node", feature, threshold, left, right); a leaf is ("leaf", count).

def build_tree(data, depth, max_depth, rng):
    n = len(data)
    if depth >= max_depth or n <= 1:
        return ("leaf", n)
    j = rng.randint(data.shape[1])
    lo, hi = data[:, j].min(), data[:, j].max()
    if lo == hi:
        return ("leaf", n)
    split = rng.uniform(lo, hi)
    mask = data[:, j] < split
    return ("node", j, split,
            build_tree(data[mask], depth + 1, max_depth, rng),
            build_tree(data[~mask], depth + 1, max_depth, rng))

tree_path drops one point down a tree and returns its path length. At a leaf it returns the depth reached plus c_factor(count), since a leaf holding several points means the tree stopped before isolating them. At a node it goes left or right and adds 1 to the depth. A short path is the sign of an anomaly.

def tree_path(x, node, depth=0):
    if node[0] == "leaf":
        return depth + c_factor(node[1])
    _, j, split, left, right = node
    return tree_path(x, left if x[j] < split else right, depth + 1)

forest_scores uses sub-samples of at most 256 points (psi), grows trees to ceil(log2(psi)) depth, builds n_trees trees on different random sub-samples, averages each point's path length, and scores it as 2 ** (−depth / c_factor(psi)).

def forest_scores(X, n_trees=100, psi=256, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.RandomState(seed)
    n = len(X); psi = min(psi, n)
    max_depth = int(np.ceil(np.log2(psi)))
    trees = [build_tree(X[rng.choice(n, psi, replace=False)], 0, max_depth, rng)
             for _ in range(n_trees)]
    depth = np.array([np.mean([tree_path(x, t) for t in trees]) for x in X])
    return 2 ** (-depth / c_factor(psi)), depth

score_mine, depth_mine = forest_scores(Xc)

Weakness: axis-parallel splits

Every split is parallel to an axis, so the score contours show rectangular banding. Two points at the same distance from the data can get different scores depending on whether they sit along an axis direction or diagonally off it. This is most visible for diagonal structure like the Mileage and Price band. Variants such as the Extended Isolation Forest use randomly rotated cuts to reduce it.

Part 5

One-Class SVM: draw a boundary around "normal"

A One-Class classifier is trained on data from a single class (here, normal listings) and learns a boundary around it. Anything outside is an anomaly.

The bubble

Picture the smallest "bubble" that holds most normal points. We let some points sit outside (suspected outliers), paying a penalty (slack) for each. A tight bubble is small but many points pay slack. A loose bubble has no slack but swallows the outliers.

The kernel trick

With an RBF kernel, the bubble lives in a higher-dimensional space. Drawn back in the original plane it can take any curved shape, which suits non-spherical data like our diagonal band.
ParameterMeaning
nuAn upper bound on the fraction of training points treated as outliers and a lower bound on the fraction of support vectors. Roughly: the share you are willing to flag.
gammaHow local the RBF kernel is. Small gives a smooth, loose boundary. Large gives a wiggly boundary hugging individual points (overfitting).

Scale the features first. An RBF kernel is based on distances, so a feature with big numbers dominates unless we standardise.

Interactive · what gamma does (schematic)

On DriveDeal. Small gamma (0.05) is smooth and loose, and can include mild anomalies. Large gamma (5) wraps individual points and may flag ordinary listings in the thin parts of the band. A medium gamma follows the diagonal band without chasing points. gamma="scale" (default) is a sensible start. The number flagged stays close to nu times the number of listings.
Code: scaling and three gamma values
scaler = StandardScaler().fit(Xc)
Xs = scaler.transform(Xc)
grid_s = scaler.transform(grid_pts)

for g in [0.05, 0.5, 5]:
    m = OneClassSVM(kernel="rbf", gamma=g, nu=CONTAM).fit(Xs)
    flagged = m.predict(Xs) == -1
    zz = m.decision_function(grid_s).reshape(xx.shape)

Limitations

  • The kernel and gamma matter a lot, and there are no labels to tune them against.
  • Training time grows quickly with the number of points, so it does not scale to millions of rows.
  • It needs scaled features, and inherits the usual SVM quirks.
Part 6

Local Outlier Factor: unusual compared with your neighbours

Suppose DriveDeal has a very tight group of mass-market hatchbacks (priced almost the same) and a loose group of luxury cars (prices spread widely). A listing priced a little away from the hatchbacks is odd for hatchbacks, even though the same gap would be normal among luxury cars. A global distance threshold cannot tell the two apart. We need a local notion of "unusual".

LOF's idea: compare each point's local density with the local density of its neighbours. A point in a sparse spot surrounded by dense neighbours is an anomaly.

Building blocks (k = number of neighbours)

Step explorer

The distance from A to its k-th nearest neighbour. A large k-distance means the point is far from others.

reach-distk(A, B) = max{ k-distance(B), d(A, B) }

This smooths out tiny distances. If A is very close to B, we still count at least B's own neighbourhood size.

lrdk(A) = ( (1/k) · ΣB∈Nk(A) reach-distk(A, B) )−1

The inverse of the average reachability distance from A to its k neighbours.

LOFk(A) = [ (1/k) · ΣB∈Nk(A) lrdk(B) ] / lrdk(A)

The average lrd of A's neighbours divided by A's own lrd.

Interactive · how to read an LOF value

There is no universal cut-off. A value just above 1 on the edge of a dense region is normal, whereas something like 2.7 usually deserves a look. The threshold is set by the use case and domain experts.

LOF from scratch: P and Q

A test dataset has a tight cluster (150 points around (0, 0)), a loose cluster (150 points around (6, 0)), and two special points: P, 1.0 away from the tight cluster, and Q, far above the loose cluster. We rank all points (rank 1 = most anomalous) by a global measure (k-distance alone) and by LOF, with K = 10.

Rank comparison · lower rank = more anomalous

P looks unremarkable by k-distance (rank 49), because the loose cluster has many points with larger k-distances. LOF ranks it first (LOF = 5.22), because its neighbours are far denser than it is.

Q is far from everything, so both measures flag it (LOF = 4.39).

Takeaway. Comparing with local density catches P, which "far from everything" misses. Our implementation matches scikit-learn's with a largest difference of about 3.2 × 10−9.
Code: LOF from scratch
def lof_scratch(X, k):
    D = squareform(pdist(X)); n = len(X)
    idx = np.argsort(D, axis=1)[:, 1:k + 1]           # k nearest neighbours (skip self)
    rows = np.arange(n)[:, None]
    kdist = D[np.arange(n), idx[:, -1]]               # k-distance
    reach = np.maximum(D[rows, idx], kdist[idx])      # reachability distance
    lrd = 1.0 / reach.mean(axis=1)                    # local reachability density
    return lrd[idx].mean(axis=1) / lrd, kdist         # LOF and k-distance

rs = np.random.RandomState(1)
tight = rs.normal([0, 0], 0.15, (150, 2))
loose = rs.normal([6, 0], 1.2, (150, 2))
P, Q = np.array([[1.0, 0.0]]), np.array([[6.0, 5.5]])
pts = np.vstack([tight, loose, P, Q]); iP, iQ = len(pts) - 2, len(pts) - 1

K = 10
lof_mine, kdist = lof_scratch(pts, K)
sk = LocalOutlierFactor(n_neighbors=K).fit(pts)

Part 7

Elliptic Envelope and a fair comparison

Elliptic Envelope is a simple parametric method. It fits a robust Gaussian (a robust covariance estimate not pulled by outliers) and flags points with a large Mahalanobis distance, i.e. points outside an ellipse around the data. It works when normal data forms one blob, and fails for several blobs, curves or very different densities, because one ellipse cannot represent them.

The experiment

Instead of judging by pictures, five toy datasets get planted outliers, so the truth is known. Methods are scored by AUC: how well anomaly scores rank true outliers above normal points (1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is chance). Every method gets the same flag budget, 15%.

  • One-Class SVM can follow curved shapes, but depends on gamma and scale.

Part 8

Back to DriveDeal: a consensus review queue

No single method is always right, and reviewers have limited time. So Rohan combines the methods: run four detectors with the same budget, count how many flag each listing (its votes, 0 to 4), and put listings with more votes at the top.

Code: fit, vote and queue
ee = EllipticEnvelope(contamination=CONTAM, random_state=0).fit(Xs)
ocsvm = OneClassSVM(kernel="rbf", gamma="scale", nu=CONTAM).fit(Xs)

flags = {
  "Isolation Forest":  flag_if,
  "One-Class SVM":     ocsvm.predict(Xs) == -1,
  "LOF":               flag_lof,
  "Elliptic Envelope": ee.predict(Xs) == -1,
}
votes = sum(f.astype(int) for f in flags.values())

# Jaccard overlap of each pair of flagged sets
jac = pd.DataFrame([[(flags[a] & flags[b]).sum() / max((flags[a] | flags[b]).sum(), 1)
                     for b in names] for a in names], index=names, columns=names)

queue = data.assign(votes=votes, if_score=-iso.score_samples(Xc)) \
            .sort_values(["votes", "if_score"], ascending=False)

How stable is the ranking?

A good detector should not change its mind because of the random seed. Rerun Isolation Forest with several seeds (five here) and measure the overlap of the top-flagged listings.

Part 9

Screening new listings (novelty detection)

Each new listing must be checked against what DriveDeal already knows. The reference data is the existing catalogue. Fit on 80% of the listings (726) and score the held-out 20% (182), plus three hand-made listings. LOF needs novelty=True to score points it was not fitted on.

No leakage. The scaler learns its mean and standard deviation from the reference listings only. Learning the scaling from new data would leak information a real incoming listing would not have. Isolation Forest works on raw values, while the distance-based methods get scaled input.

Practical advice

  • Start with Isolation Forest and LOF. Add the others for a second opinion.
  • Use one shared budget, scale features for distance-based methods, and look at rankings, not only flags.
  • Combine methods with votes, check stability, and let reviewers' verdicts become your first labels.
  • Decide whether you are doing outlier or novelty detection before you pick the API.
